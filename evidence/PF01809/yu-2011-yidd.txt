Figure 5: separate processing and membrane-abundance readouts
Deletion replaces almost all yidD coding sequence in MC4100(DE3), with
kanamycin-cassette construction and PCR verification, while retaining
the overlapping rnpA ORF. The paper reports no discernible growth or
morphology defect in its tested conditions; much of that evidence is
not shown. This does not prove universal dispensability.
YidC abundance is similar in WT and deletion in Figure 5A, with Lep
as control. No same-gene complementation is shown for the adopted effects.

Cells carrying pCL-M13P2 or pCL-CyoA-HA are induced in early log phase
with 100 uM IPTG for 1 h; LB is the default medium in Methods.
Figure 5B: mature M13P2/(mature + precursor) is 89 +/- 2.1% in WT and
69 +/- 3.9% in the deletion. Lep is the loading comparator.
Figure 5C: mature CyoA-HA fraction is 43 +/- 2.6% in WT and
26 +/- 3.2% in the deletion. OmpA processing/loading is the comparator
and appears unchanged. Four biological replicates are stated for the
processing fractions; the caption does not identify the +/- statistic.
These are steady-state processing fractions, not insertion-rate constants.
Changes in processing, abundance or turnover are not independently
resolved by these blots, even though the authors interpret them in
the context of YidC-dependent insertion.
Figure 5D: F0c signal in deletion-derived inner membrane vesicles is
labeled 48% of WT, with Lep control. Replicate number/error estimate
for that panel is not given. It measures steady-state membrane abundance,
not a direct F0c insertion or assembled ATP synthase activity assay.

Figure 4: fusion-localization intervention
MC4100-A carrying pEH3GFP, pEH3GFP-YidD or pEH3GFP-YidDH1 is grown in
M9 and induced at OD660 approximately 0.3 with 50 uM IPTG for 1 h,
then fixed for phase-contrast and fluorescence microscopy. GFP alone
is diffuse; both YidD fusions show peripheral/halo fluorescence.
H1 is residues 1-27, not the complete PF01809 interval 10-76.
The phenotype is qualitative peripheral enrichment, not calibrated
membrane affinity, isolated lipid binding or insertion across a bilayer.
Expression may exceed endogenous YidD. No H1-loss mutant establishing
necessity is presented here. Urea-resistant association is reported
without shown data and is not promoted to direct lipid-binding chemistry.

Figure 2C: tagged-protein induction
MC4100-A with pEH3His-YidD grows in LB, induction at OD660 0.3 with
1 mM IPTG for 1 h. Anti-His signal appears and PspA signal modestly
increases relative to the uninduced preparation. YidC signal is not
detectably altered. This is the joint IPTG/tagged-expression intervention,
not proof that physiological YidD directly activates PspA transcription.
An IPTG-only empty-vector control is not displayed in this panel.
Do not borrow the 100-uM dose of the separate fractionation experiment.

Association and models, not adopted directed biological edges
His-YidD membrane fractionation and detergent extraction support membrane
association but show recovery/instability limitations. Figure 3A caption
says pEH3His-YidC where the surrounding experiment concerns His-YidD.
Nascent 108FtsQ Cys15 yields a His-YidD-containing BMOE adduct; Cys36/61
do not. This establishes proximity in an engineered arrested intermediate,
not activation of FtsQ insertion, direct YidC recruitment or ribosome
tethering. The authors report unsuccessful translocon/ribosome pulldowns
without shown data. Chaperone and tethering functions remain hypotheses.
Genomic proximity, co-transcription and predicted helices are not causal
biochemical steps. Hemolysin labels and other taxa need independent review.
