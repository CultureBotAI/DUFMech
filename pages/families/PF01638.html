Source description

HxlR, a member of this family, is a DNA-binding protein that acts as a positive regulator of the formaldehyde-inducible hxlAB operon in Bacillus subtilis.

Formaldehyde produces a plus-12-Da species of purified intact HxlR

MECHANISTIC

Scope
Purified B. subtilis 168 HxlR expressed from pET28a in E. coli BL21(DE3), Zhu 2021 Supplementary Figure 1c; not the synthetic peptide, cellular MS/MS or FAsor fusion assay.
Context
Incubate 100 uM HxlR with 1 mM formaldehyde in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5 and 300 mM NaCl for 1 h at 37 C; desalt by Micro Bio-Spin 6 and analyze by Bruker Solarix XR FT-MS. Purification removes the N-terminal His tag with thrombin, followed by DTT treatment and SEC. The structural preparation retains a GSH cleavage extension.
Family mapping
P42406 sequence version 3 is 120 aa; UniProt/InterPro sequences match, with PF01638 at 19-107. PDB 7BZD entity 1 is exactly GSH plus this reference. C11 and K13 are outside the current Pfam interval. The experiment therefore concerns an intact domain-bearing protein, not chemistry by the isolated Pfam domain.
Limitations
Intact-mass spectra establish new plus-12-Da species, not their unique chemical topology alone. Separate crystal density, cellular tryptic MS/MS and synthetic-peptide substitutions support the C11/K13 methylene assignment. Those samples are not one time-resolved reaction trajectory. Unmodified and sodium-adduct species persist; no quantitative yield, chemical reversibility or obligatory sequential intermediate is asserted.

Formaldehyde enhances WT HxlR DNA binding, with little enhancement in C11A

MECHANISTIC

Scope
Purified pET28a-derived WT and C11A HxlR, Zhu 2021 Figure 1e; separate from cellular reporter and K13A titrations.
Context
EMSA Methods pretreat 100 uM protein with or without 1 mM formaldehyde for 1-2 h at 37 C in 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5 and 300 mM NaCl. Figure 1e instead labels 0.6 mM formaldehyde; this discrepancy remains unresolved. Final WT/C11A protein is 0.2, 0.4 or 0.6 uM in 20 ul with 50 nM 50-bp BRH duplex, 20 mM HEPES pH 7.0, 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2 and 10% glycerol. Table 2 specifies BRH-F/R, not the optimized 20-bp crystallization duplex. Representative gels from three independent experiments.
Family mapping
P42406 / B. subtilis 168 reference 1-120 contains PF01638 19-107; C11 is outside that interval. The N-terminal His tag is thrombin-cleaved, with GSH retained in the sequence-verified structural preparation. Neither C11A nor WT is an isolated Pfam-domain construct.
Limitations
Gel-shift enhancement is a direct biochemical binding response, not a measured transcription rate or fitted KD. C11A retains basal binding; lack of enhancement is not complete loss of DNA binding. Equal mutant folding and active fraction are not established by these gels. The formaldehyde concentration discrepancy is not resolved by assumed dilution, and static structural differences do not directly observe helix flipping.

K13A HxlR binds DNA but lacks the WT formaldehyde enhancement

MECHANISTIC

Scope
Purified K13A HxlR in Zhu 2021 Supplementary Figure 2d; its titration range differs from WT/C11A Figure 1e.
Context
Supplementary Figure 2d uses 0.05, 0.1, 0.15 and 0.2 uM K13A, with or without treatment labeled 0.6 mM formaldehyde. Shared EMSA Methods specify 1 mM pretreatment of 100 uM protein for 1-2 h at 37 C, followed by dilution into 50 nM BRH duplex with HEPES pH 7.0, KCl, MgCl2 and glycerol. The formaldehyde discrepancy is unresolved. Representative gel from three independent experiments; the WT positive response is from the separate Figure 1e range.
Family mapping
K13 is verified in P42406 and lies outside PF01638 19-107. The intact pET28a-derived HxlR mutant is tested, not the isolated HxlR-like domain. The 7BZD sequence check concerns WT structural protein, not an independently resequenced K13A assay stock.
Limitations
The edge denotes loss of the WT treatment response, not lower absolute DNA binding at identical WT/K13A concentrations. K13A retains strong basal shifts and the authors describe higher basal binding. Different concentration ranges do not establish a quantitative affinity ratio. Directness is confined to the biochemical response; a unique structural intermediate or conformational trajectory is not demonstrated.

HxlR substitutions reduce the formaldehyde-induced GFP reporter response in E. coli

PHENOTYPIC

Scope
E. coli BW25113 carrying pBAD-HxlR constructs labeled WT, C11A or K13A and a B. subtilis 168 hxlAB-promoter GFP reporter, Zhu 2021 Figure 1f and Supplementary Figure 2e.
Context
Dilute overnight cultures 1:100 into LB, grow to OD600 0.6, induce HxlR with 4 mM L-arabinose for 1 h, then treat with or without 600 uM formaldehyde for 40 min. Measure GFP by flow cytometry at 488-nm excitation and 530/30BP emission. Supplementary Figure 2b/c provides colony-count viability controls and scatter gating. These are not the purified pET28a constructs or native B. subtilis cultures.
Family mapping
The source gene is P42406 from B. subtilis 168, with current PF01638 19-107; C11 and K13 are outside that interval. Supplementary Table 2's pBAD forward primer is compatible with an MGRM start instead of reference MSRM if translated from its apparent start, but Methods describe Gibson assembly. A primer-implied S2G possibility remains unresolved; author WT/C11A/K13A labels do not prove exact native N-terminal sequence.
Limitations
GFP distributions are indirect reporter phenotypes, not direct transcription-rate, formaldehyde-detoxification or native-operon measurements. C11A has lower basal reporter signal, whereas K13A has higher basal signal and little further induction. Viability/gating controls do not establish equal mutant protein abundance, folding or localization. The pBAD sequence discrepancy is preserved rather than claiming a resequenced WT plasmid. Separate biochemical and cellular assays are not joined into one observed trajectory.
